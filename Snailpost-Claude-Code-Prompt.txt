1. THE PRODUCT STORY

Product name: Snailpost.
Primary line: "A day in my life, as a postcard."
Supporting line: "Little moments. A little closer."

Start with a specific audience: college students and young adults living far
from home who want to share everyday life with a parent or grandparent in
another time zone, especially someone who prefers familiar messaging apps
over learning another social platform.

This audience is a starting hypothesis, not a claim that we have conducted
research. Age does not determine technical ability. Design for preferences,
readability, and the relationship between sender and recipient.

The job to be done:
"When our schedules don't line up, help me turn a few ordinary moments into
something personal my family can open, hear, and keep, without arranging a call
or performing for a social feed."

The problem is not simply sending another photo. Small details arrive scattered,
calls require coordination, and an ordinary day can feel too unimportant to
share. Snailpost gives those details a beginning, a sequence, a voice, and an
object worth keeping.

The core ritual:
- During the day, collect one to six moments.
- In the evening, arrange them into a postcard and add a short note or voice.
- Share a link through an existing messaging app.
- A relative opens it whenever convenient, listens, turns it over, and can
  print it for the fridge or a keepsake box.

The emotional peak is a relative recognizing the sender in an ordinary detail:
"I made your eggs this morning. They still weren't as good as yours."

Make it feel like a small piece of home crossing a distance.

2. PRODUCT BOUNDARIES AND PRIORITIES

Build this first:
- Capture or upload photos; add short captions; reorder and remove moments.
- Restore an unfinished draft after refresh.
- Assemble an attractive postcard from those actual moments.
- Write an editable note and optionally record up to 60 seconds of voice.
- Preview the front and back with a satisfying card flip.
- Browse keepsakes as a tactile postcard cluster, with an accessible list option.
- Offer a playful creator interface and a calm recipient interface.
- Publish a durable, unlisted link when a backend is configured.
- Share through the device share sheet, with a copy-link fallback.
- Open the published card on another device without recipient signup.
- Export a printable postcard PDF.
- Keep a small collection of drafts and published postcards.
- Include a clearly identified sample story for reliable demonstrations.

After the complete core journey works, consider PNG export and a tiny "Send a
little love back" acknowledgement. These are optional and must not delay the
core loop.

Defer automatic translation, translated speech in the sender's voice,
AI-generated illustrations of personal photos, scheduled delivery, physical
mail fulfillment, contact imports, subscriptions, and a native app-store build.

The first version assembles real photos with original decorative illustrations.
It must not pretend that photos have been AI-redrawn or that text has been
translated. Original voice recording is part of the core; voice cloning is not.

Sharing is user initiated. "They wake up to your day" describes asynchronous
use, not an automatic messaging or scheduling integration.

There is no public feed, follower count, streak, competitive stamp collection,
mandatory daily reminder, or pressure to reply. One moment is enough.

3. VISUAL DIRECTION: ONE BRAND, TWO EXPERIENCES

The moodboard contains layered letters, paper scraps, botanical ephemera,
postage stamps, handwritten notes, irregular snail spirals, colorful painted
snails, Peach Pit lettering, and the earthy Pinefolk/Tierra references.

Translate those qualities into a coherent product. Create original Snailpost
assets rather than tracing the reference logos or reusing unlicensed artwork.

The organizing idea is "a tiny postal service for everyday life."

Shared brand:
- Warm paper, generous margins, dark ink, tangible card edges.
- One recognizable spiral snail mark that also works as a small favicon.
- A lowercase wordmark with an organic, slightly imperfect personality.
- A consistent postcard object, stamp system, and envelope language.
- Gentle visual imperfections in artwork; precise alignment in the controls.

Use this starting palette as design tokens, refining combinations after actual
contrast checks:
- Paper: #F6F1E7
- Ink: #2B2925
- Peach: #F2C2AE
- Clay: #B54532
- Moss: #3D513E
- Marigold: #D4A24A
- Postal blue: #2C4C9D

Pastels are surfaces and accents. Keep functional text dark and legible.
Avoid glass effects, neon gradients, dashboard widgets, and generic SaaS
styling. Paper texture should be subtle and should never interfere with text.

A. "Postcard Studio" — the creator experience

This is the more playful, Peach Pit-inspired side: peach and butter-colored
paper, rounded organic headlines, imperfect stamps, small doodles, and a few
photo corners or pieces of tape. Imagine an evening at a bedroom desk.

Use a licensed organic display face or an appropriate open-source substitute
such as Fraunces, paired with a readable UI sans. Any handwriting face is for
short decorative annotations only. Do not place essential instructions in
difficult handwriting.

The composition can feel handmade, while the navigation remains orderly.
Do not rotate every panel or put a sticker on every control.

B. "Easy Reading" — the recipient experience

Take the quiet, earthy qualities of Pinefolk and Tierra: cream, moss, restrained
clay, generous whitespace, and clear typography. Organic display type is an
accent; instructions, dates, and messages use a highly readable face.

Use approximately 18–20 px body text, comfortable line spacing, large labeled
controls, and minimal navigation. Put the sender, the date, and the next action
where they are immediately understandable.

This should feel like opening a thoughtful letter. Never call it "elderly mode."

Creators can choose a calmer interface. Recipients can increase text size and
choose either appearance. Shared links default to Easy Reading. These are
presentation choices over the same content, not separate apps or accounts.

Original asset direction:
- A simple snail with an envelope-shaped shell detail, readable at small sizes.
- Four initial stamps: Breakfast Club, On My Way, Little Victories, and Home
  Again. Each represents a moment category, not a reward or a streak.
- A tiny sprig, sun, steam curl, and postal cancellation mark.
- A quiet envelope illustration for the opening state.

Prefer compact SVG assets for the simple original brand marks and decorative
illustrations. Keep photos and postcard content separate from decoration.
Record asset provenance and any font licenses.

4. SCREEN-BY-SCREEN EXPERIENCE

Use a working mobile layout around 390 px as the design baseline, but support
320 px through desktop without horizontal overflow. On larger screens, give
the creator a comfortable two-column workspace with a live card preview.
Do not put the entire product inside a fake phone frame.

A. First visit / Today's moments

Open into the product, not a long marketing website.

Show the Snailpost mark, a small date, a recipient chip, and:
"What would you like to send home today?"

Primary action: "Add a little moment."
Secondary action: "Explore a sample postcard."

Ask for a recipient display name or relationship when useful. No address book
permission or street address is needed. Let someone start collecting before
they create a sender account; explain any sign-in required to publish when
they reach that step.

A growing, gently fanned cluster of photo scraps shows today's moments. Use
time labels such as "8:12 am" and short captions. A small envelope preview
quietly fills as moments are added.

Empty-state copy:
"Nothing big has to happen. Breakfast counts."

After at least one moment exists, show "Make today's postcard."
Use three clear destinations: Today, Keepsakes, and Settings.

B. Add a moment

Support taking a photo when the browser allows it and choosing from the photo
library. Provide an optional caption prompt:
"Tell them the bit the photo can't."

Allow review, replacement, removal, and reordering. Include tap-based move
controls so drag gestures are optional. Show upload or local-save progress
honestly. Handle large photos and unsupported file formats with useful recovery.

Limit each card to six moments to keep the story short. A single photo must
still create an intentional, beautiful card.

Do not require continuous camera access, background recording, or location
tracking. Place names are optional and manually entered.

C. Compose the postcard

Use a clear three-step flow: Moments → Your note → Preview.

Offer one polished default layout with restrained alternatives only if needed.
The user should not have to become a graphic designer.

For one photo, make an intimate hero image. For two to four, use a deliberate
collage with breathing room. For five or six, use a clean sequence. Preserve
user-defined ordering and use that same ordering in the reader and exports.

A short title and note are editable. Start with the user's own captions where
possible. A deterministic layout engine is sufficient for this version:
arrange content, add decorative marks, and preserve the user's wording.
Do not invent what happened, emotions, relationships, or quotes.

Keep the main postcard note short, around 280 characters. Preserve longer
supporting captions and any transcript in the accessible reading view.

Optional voice:
"Tell it in your voice."
Offer Record, Stop, Listen back, Record again, and Remove. Show elapsed time.
If the microphone is unavailable or permission is denied, keep the written
postcard fully usable and offer an audio-file upload where supported.

Always include readable written content. If an actual transcription integration
is configured, show its result for correction before publishing. Otherwise
offer a manually entered transcript or clearly labeled written summary.
Never label a fabricated or approximate summary as a verbatim transcript.

Finish by choosing one stamp. The default should already look good.

D. Preview and the signature card flip

This is the signature interaction. Inspect the exact Ramp sketchbook reference:
https://x.com/jeanxcrj/status/2064835647152787800

Translate its intended sketchbook tactility into a convincing, reversible,
two-sided postcard. If a suitable source component exists in the repository,
inspect it before writing a replacement. Do not reduce this to an unrelated
stock hover-flip just because that is easier to implement.

Proposed adaptation, to refine after viewing the reference: the postcard lifts
slightly from the desk, turns with a controlled edge-led pivot and a traveling
shadow, then settles. Let depth, edge thickness, and easing communicate paper.
Use restrained flex only if it can be implemented cleanly; a heavyweight 3D
engine is not required. The object stays a postcard, not a bound notebook.

Front:
- The illustrated photo composition.
- The postcard title and date.
- A small sender signature.
- One restrained decorative detail.

Back:
- "Dear [recipient]," and the short message.
- "Love, [sender]."
- The selected stamp, a postal divider, and a postmark.
- Optional city-to-city text if the user supplied it.

Use a landscape postcard proportion, roughly 3:2, with paper-colored edges
and a soft directional shadow. Avoid shrinking text to force everything into
the visual card: show the full note and captions below when necessary.

The flip should feel like turning cardstock:
- Approximately 550–700 ms for a deliberate half turn.
- Perspective and a restrained change of shadow during the turn.
- Correct front and back orientation with no mirrored text.
- No continuous spinning, automatic flipping, or movement while reading.

Provide persistent, labeled controls: "Read the back" and "See the front."
A direct tap can be an additional affordance; hover and swipe must never be
the only way to operate it. Keyboard activation must work.

Use proper hidden-face semantics and focus handling. Do not leave hidden
buttons in the tab order. Avoid wrapping all interactive card content inside
one large button.

Honor reduced-motion settings by switching faces instantly or with a brief
fade. Keep the layout stable, including when content or text size changes.
Provide a "Read as a letter" view with all content in normal document flow.

Main action: "Ready to share."
Secondary action: "Keep editing."

E. Share

Explain the two ways to keep a postcard:
- "Send a link" for photos, note, and voice.
- "Print a keepsake" for a physical card.

Publish a snapshot after the sender reviews it, then create its durable link.
A later draft edit must not silently alter something already shared; require
an explicit update, or create a new postcard version.

Use the native share sheet when available. Offer "Copy link" as a visible
fallback. Support the user's existing messaging app through the operating
system rather than requiring a new conversation inside Snailpost.

Do not claim automatic delivery to WhatsApp, Messages, WeChat, or any other
app. Share-sheet success does not prove the recipient received or opened it.
Use truthful states such as "Link ready" and "Link copied."

Offer a plain-language sharing note:
"Anyone with this link can open your postcard."
Let the sender disable the link later.

Treat canceling the share sheet as normal, not a red error state.
Keep unsent content intact if publishing or upload fails.

F. Recipient opening

The shared page must work in a fresh, unauthenticated browser on another
device. No app installation, signup wall, or creator navigation.

Start with:
"A little of [sender]'s day, for you."
Show the date and one prominent "Open postcard" button.

Use one short envelope-opening transition, then let the story breathe.
A repeat visit should not force the recipient through a lengthy animation.

Offer:
- "Play [sender]'s voice" when an audio recording exists.
- "Read the back" / "See the front."
- "Read as a letter."
- A simple larger-text setting.
- "Print this postcard."

Never autoplay audio. Include pause, replay, duration, and a readable transcript
or clearly labeled written summary. If audio cannot play, explain that gently
and keep the photos and message available.

The letter view should present the moments in order, with captions and the
note, as the accessible alternative to the compact card. Do not hide the story
behind animation or tiny artwork.

G. Keepsakes

A calm, small collection of cards organized by date and recipient. Distinguish
drafts from published cards. Avoid "delivered" or "read" labels without a real
delivery or receipt integration.

Support reopening, sharing again, downloading, disabling a published link,
and deleting with an appropriate confirmation. Keep the visual metaphor of
a keepsake box without making navigation obscure.

H. The postcard cluster — a handful of days

Use the ticket-card project as the explicit interaction reference:
https://github.com/ela1ne0/ticket-card

Proposed Snailpost cluster behavior:
- Resting: three to five visible postcard edges form a loose, shallow fan on
  warm paper. Small deterministic rotations, around -6 to +6 degrees, give
  personality without making titles hard to read. Group by month or recipient.
- Browse: selecting "Browse postcards" spreads the group enough to choose a
  card. Desktop hover may gently lift a visible card; touch and keyboard
  selection must provide the same essential access.
- Select: a chosen postcard rises and moves into a centered reading position.
  The other cards recede slightly so the selected story becomes the focus.
- Read: the selected postcard supports the Ramp-inspired front/back turn.
  Opening a card and turning it over are distinct actions.
- Return: "Back to keepsakes" returns to the same group, scroll position,
  and selected-card context, with focus restored to the triggering control.

The intended visual sequence is: a handful of days → choose one → lift it
out → turn it over → put it back. This makes the archive feel like a set of
objects someone kept, and connects the cluster directly to storytelling.

On phones, keep the stack inside the viewport. Provide a visible title/date
and Previous/Next controls or a clear accessible list so a narrow exposed
card sliver is never someone's only target. Limit rendered animated cards;
do not build an infinite physics playground. Keep rotations stable on rerender.

Do not use the cluster for form fields, settings, or the default recipient
page. Easy Reading opens one card at a time and defaults to the straightforward
list if browsing a collection. Keep "Next postcard" distinct from "Read the
back" so navigating a collection cannot be confused with turning one card.

Implement explicit states for resting, browsing, selected-front, selected-back,
and returning. Animate transforms and opacity where possible. Handle rapid
taps, interrupted transitions, resizing, and stale selections without ghost
cards, duplicate focus targets, or a stuck overlay. If a modal is used, give
it correct dialog semantics, Escape behavior, and focus restoration.

Suggested motion starting points: 180–250 ms for a small lift, 300–450 ms for
selection/return, and 550–700 ms for the postcard turn. These are design
proposals, not measurements from the references. Reduced-motion mode keeps
all states and controls with immediate changes or brief fades.

6. IMPLEMENTATION AND DATA INTEGRITY

If starting from scratch, use Next.js with TypeScript, a token-based styling
system such as Tailwind/CSS variables, and lightweight motion. Target Vercel.
Use compatible, currently supported package versions, a committed lockfile,
and documented setup. If an existing suitable stack is present, prefer
extending it over unnecessary replacement.

Suggested routes; adapt them to repository conventions:
- /today
- /postcards/[id]/edit
- /postcards/[id]/preview
- /keepsakes
- /p/[token]
- /demo

Use reusable components for the postcard faces, accessible card flip, moment
capture, voice recorder/player, stamps, theme controls, share actions, and
print layout, plus a reusable PostcardCluster with explicit selection state.
Use one postcard content model across creator, recipient,
letter view, and export so these representations do not drift apart.

Suggested entities:
- Recipient: display name and optional place; language preference can be
  added when translation exists.
- Moment: id, order, photo reference, caption, and captured timestamp.
- Draft: owner/local id, recipient, moments, title, note, appearance, audio,
  written transcript/summary status, and save state.
- PublishedPostcard: an immutable content snapshot, version, owner, creation
  date, asset references, and active/revoked state.
- ShareLink: a cryptographically random token, its protected server-side
  representation, the postcard version, and revocation state.

For a real shared link, use durable server-side data and media storage.
Supabase Auth, Postgres, and private Storage are a reasonable default if
those services are available. Existing equivalent infrastructure is fine.
Do not pretend localStorage, in-memory objects, or the Vercel deployment
filesystem can make personal postcards available on a second device.

Store local draft media in IndexedDB or another appropriate local persistence
layer. Do not put large base64 photo blobs in localStorage. Preserve drafts
through refresh and migration into an authenticated sender account.

Use minimal sender authentication for real publishing and ownership. Recipients
remain unauthenticated link viewers. Backend credentials stay on the server.
Enforce ownership for creator reads, edits, uploads, deletes, and link controls;
another sender must not be able to access private drafts or modify a card.

Use private media storage. Validate an active share token before granting
recipient access to a published snapshot and its media. Do not expose bucket
listing or use public media URLs as a shortcut. Link tokens must be high
entropy, not sequential ids. Keep tokens and private content out of analytics
and error logs.

Use appropriate no-store behavior on private/shared content, noindex headers,
a no-referrer policy on bearer-link pages, and a generic link preview that
does not reveal private photos. Account for the expiry window of any signed
media URLs when documenting what link revocation guarantees.

Validate image/audio size, type, and authorization on the server as well as
the client. Handle image orientation, resize sensibly, remove unnecessary
location metadata from processed images, and clean up abandoned uploads.
Support common phone formats where practical; explicitly handle unsupported
HEIC instead of accepting a file that later disappears.

Use MediaRecorder feature detection and supported MIME-type negotiation.
Stop media tracks on completion or cancellation. Release temporary object
URLs. Do not assume one recording codec works on every phone or browser.

Save dates with a clear time zone; display the sender's day consistently.
Optional destination time-zone information must not imply scheduled delivery.

Implement idempotent publishing so a double tap or network retry does not
create duplicate cards. Preserve a successful publication if sharing is
canceled. Track honest states such as draft, saving, ready, publishing,
published, and failed.

If backend credentials are unavailable, build the complete local creation,
preview, recording, and export experience plus a bundled public sample.
Label the sample/local preview accurately. Do not create a fake share URL
for a personal card. Prepare the schema, policies, environment-variable
template, and integration steps, and mark cross-device publishing BLOCKED
until it is genuinely configured and tested.

The interface should explain a user's next action in plain language; keep
provider names and environment setup in developer documentation.

7. PRINTING AND THE PHYSICAL KEEPSAKE

Printing is a core expression of the product, not a decorative extra.

Provide a downloadable two-page PDF with one 6 × 4 inch landscape face per
page: front on page one, back on page two. Use a clear safe margin, readable
type, and embedded images/fonts. At 300 dpi, a full-face raster export would
be 1800 × 1200 px; retain vector type where practical.

Keep the PDF bound to the same reviewed postcard snapshot. Do not include
browser controls, 3D transforms, shadows outside the card, or clipped text.
Use a static print rendering path rather than a screenshot of the animated UI.

Include concise instructions to print at actual size and check the printer's
duplex orientation. Do not promise that every home printer will align both
faces perfectly.

For a published card with voice, offer an optional QR code and short caption:
"Scan to hear my day." Explain that the QR provides access to the same
unlisted postcard. Generate it from the canonical public share URL and verify
that it decodes to that exact URL. Local-only drafts must not print unusable
localhost or blob URLs.

If the source photo resolution is too low, give a gentle quality notice.
If long content cannot fit, use a clearly indicated continuation page rather
than silently cropping or shrinking to illegible type; the normal short
postcard should remain two pages.

Provide a working download and, where practical, a browser print fallback.
This version creates printable files; it does not order or mail physical cards.

8. ACCESSIBILITY, RESPONSIVENESS, AND FAILURE STATES

Treat these as part of the design:
- At least WCAG AA contrast for functional content.
- Approximately 44 px minimum interactive targets; aim for 48 px in Easy Reading.
- Visible focus, semantic buttons, labels, headings, and usable tab order.
- Screen-reader access to photos, captions, notes, audio controls, and status.
- No information conveyed only by color, sound, animation, or icon shape.
- Usable text enlargement and reflow at narrow widths.
- Respect reduced motion and never require a gesture.
- Comfortable safe-area spacing and an on-screen keyboard that does not
  cover essential fields or buttons.

Include deliberate states for empty content, loading, local-save failure,
upload interruption, microphone denial, unsupported recording, failed audio,
offline use, missing backend configuration, invalid/revoked links, canceled
sharing, and failed publication.

Use real progress where measurable. Preserve the user's work on recoverable
errors. Avoid destructive blanket cache-clearing as a recovery technique.

9. TEST THE WORK — DO NOT ONLY WRITE TEST FILES

Run the application and execute the relevant checks. Fix failures and rerun
the affected tests. Do not say "tested" because the test file exists.

Use the repository's package manager. Provide and run appropriate commands
for lint, type checking, meaningful unit/integration tests, a production build,
and Playwright end-to-end tests. Record the exact commands and results.

A successful build alone is insufficient. Cover the behaviors that protect
the experience:

Creation and persistence:
- Add four images, caption them, reorder and remove one, refresh, and verify
  that the remaining media, note, and order survive.
- Build an intentional card from one image.
- Validate corrupt, unsupported, and oversized uploads with recoverable errors.
- Deny microphone access and verify the text-only flow remains usable.

Postcard and accessibility:
- Flip front/back with pointer and keyboard; verify hidden-face focus behavior.
- Verify reduced-motion behavior and the linear letter view.
- Select, turn, and return cards from the cluster on touch and keyboard; test
  rapid repeated actions, resize, focus restoration, and the list alternative.
- Verify that browsing to the next postcard never turns the current one over,
  and that changing faces never unexpectedly changes the selected postcard.
- Check both appearances at narrow phone, standard phone, and desktop widths,
  including enlarged text.
- Run an automated accessibility scan and manually inspect the essential
  keyboard and reading flows. Do not treat an automated scan as complete proof.

Voice:
- Test record/stop/replay/remove states and actual saved audio where the
  environment supports recording.
- Use deterministic media fixtures for automated tests, clearly marked as
  fixtures. Record actual-device microphone and codec checks separately.
- Verify failed audio does not hide the written story.

Publication and permissions:
- In a configured test backend, publish a real card in browser context A.
- Open the resulting link in fresh context B with no session, cookies, local
  draft data, or mocked publication/media endpoints. Verify actual images,
  note, order, and audio load.
- Verify refresh of that recipient URL still works.
- Verify another sender cannot edit, delete, or read private draft data.
- Revoke the link and verify future access follows the documented policy.
- Double-submit publication and verify only one intended version is produced.
- Verify editing a draft does not silently change a published snapshot.

Sharing and print:
- Exercise native-share availability, unsupported browser fallback, clipboard
  success/failure, and user cancellation.
- Do not interpret share-sheet completion as delivery.
- Export and open the PDF; inspect both faces, page size, fonts, and image
  presence. Verify QR target and text overflow behavior when relevant.
- Confirm there are no blank pages, mirrored text, clipped notes, or controls
  accidentally embedded in the export.

Run browser coverage for Chromium and WebKit where available. Playwright
mobile emulation is useful but is not a substitute for testing a real iPhone
or Android share sheet, microphone, and in-app messaging browser. Record which
checks are automated, which were manual, and which remain unverified.

Use a dedicated test dataset and avoid sending messages to real contacts.
Mock external delivery boundaries as needed, but keep the actual storage and
fresh-recipient publication test real before declaring sharing complete.

Capture a small set of visual checks: Today, Compose, card front/back, Reader,
the resting and selected cluster, and print output. Inspect them and correct
overflow, weak contrast, awkward
cropping, or misaligned decoration. Keep evidence and a concise issue list.

If dependencies, credentials, browsers, or hardware prevent a test, record
BLOCKED or NOT RUN with the exact reason. Never turn a skipped test into a pass.

connection is needed. The online document remains BLOCKED until it exists.

The log must distinguish intended work from working behavior. Include:

A. Current build
- Last updated date and time.
- Commit or build identifier, if available.
- Local start instructions and verified preview/deployment URL, if available.
- One-sentence status of the complete sender-to-recipient loop.

B. Capability inventory
For each feature:
Feature | User-facing behavior | Route/component | Status | Evidence | Remaining work

Use consistent statuses:
PLANNED / IN PROGRESS / IMPLEMENTED, UNVERIFIED / VERIFIED / BLOCKED.
Indicate whether a capability uses real services, local storage, or demo fixtures.

C. Design decisions
Decision | Alternatives considered | Why chosen | Evidence or assumption

Include at least:
- One daily postcard versus a social feed.
- Playful creation versus calm reading.
- Authentic photos with illustration versus AI-redrawn memories.
- Optional voice and always-readable text.
- Link access versus recipient account creation.
- The physical keepsake and QR bridge.
- The tradeoff between tactile animation and accessibility.
- Postcard clusters versus a conventional grid, including discoverability on

Date | Build | Command or manual procedure | Environment | Result/count |
Failure or limitation | Evidence location

List actual tests executed, failures repaired, reruns, and unverified items.
Never invent counts, measurements, screenshots, research findings, or users.

E. What was created
List relevant screens, components, data models/migrations, original assets,

F. Known limitations and next steps
Keep translation, translated voice, automated scheduling, and physical mailing

11. COMPETITION STORY AND DESIGN REASONING

Alongside the code, prepare docs/demo-script.md for a 6:45 presentation:

0:00–0:40 — The human problem: "I talk to my family, but they still miss the
ordinary parts of my life."

0:40–1:10 — The specific audience and job to be done. Distinguish the concept
from a photo album or another messaging app.

1:10–3:10 — Show the complete ritual: collect moments, compose, add voice,
stamp, preview, publish, and share.

3:10–4:05 — Open the link as the recipient. Show the calm layout, card flip,
voice, readable letter view, and printable keepsake. Briefly return a card to
its cluster to show the relationship between a day and a collection.

4:05–5:20 — Explain two or three difficult design decisions. Show actual
alternatives, prototypes, or revisions produced during the work.

5:20–6:10 — Demonstrate what works, how it was tested, and the honest limits
of the current build.

6:10–6:45 — Close on the emotional payoff and the next validation step.
"A day that felt too ordinary to share became something worth keeping."

Document actual exploration as it happens. Compare at least two plausible
card compositions and two recipient entry approaches before choosing.
Do not fabricate interviews, usability results, iterations, or performance
numbers to improve the case study.

Propose, but do not claim to have completed, a small pilot with several
student–relative pairs. Useful questions:
- Can a sender create a postcard without help?
- Can a recipient open, listen, and read without coaching?
- Does the postcard communicate a more coherent day than scattered photos?
- Does either person want to keep it or receive another?

Record these as validation questions, not established outcomes.

12. BUILD ORDER AND DEFINITION OF DONE

Build in this sequence:
1. Inspect the repository and references; state the product interpretation and
   a short implementation plan.
2. Establish tokens, original brand marks, the postcard object, a realistic
   sample composition, and a small interactive cluster/flip prototype informed
   by the two supplied references.
3. Complete local capture, draft persistence, note, voice, composition, and
   preview as one working vertical slice.
4. Connect durable publishing, ownership, recipient access, and link controls
   when credentials are available.
5. Finish sharing, the readable recipient view, and print export.
6. Run functional, permission, accessibility, browser, and visual checks.
7. Refine craft, update the Google Doc/local log, and prepare the demo script.
8. Prepare a Vercel preview deployment using authorized access. If access is
   unavailable, provide precise deployment instructions and the remaining
   environment setup. Do not claim the app is live without a verified URL.

A successful handoff contains:
- Runnable code and documented setup.
- A complete, beautiful core journey with real persistence.
- A working cross-device postcard link, or an explicit blocked status with
  the local/sample capabilities accurately described.
- Accessible front/back and letter views.
- The reference-informed cluster → select → flip → return interaction, with
  a calm, accessible alternative.
- An inspected printable postcard.
- Executed test results with clear limitations.
- The actual Google Docs log URL, or a complete local fallback and its blocker.
- A concise design-decision record and a usable competition demo script.

Finish by explaining what was built, what passed, what remains blocked, how
to run it, and where to find the build log and preview.

Start now. Make the first postcard feel like someone cared enough to send it.
